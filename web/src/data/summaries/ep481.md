## Background

> Jeremy Giffon returns to Invest Like the Best after eighteen months of high-rep-count conversations across private markets — founders, fund managers, and the capital behind them. Patrick frames the discussion as advice for both sides of the table in the most uncertain environment since the internet transition.
>
> Giffon opens on venture's great filter: storytelling ability, because realized returns take a decade and interim product is narrative. He introduces the 'billion dollar PDF' — a crystallized worldview (often a deck or essay) that mobilizes billions in capital the way ten-year-olds chase a soccer ball — and links it to X's unified feed where posters, not lurkers, set daily consensus for markets, policy, and fundraising. The conversation spans cap-table creativity in bridge rounds, the death of per-seat SaaS as a business model, Mag7 as non-obvious public allocation, and a cultural thesis that attention-scarce posters are displacing billionaire worship as society's new priest class.

## Key Facts

> **F1** Narrative filter and stale company age penalty: Great private-market funds win on storytelling before returns exist. Companies seven years old with recent 200% growth but only $8M revenue get punished versus identical businesses if the clock restarted two years ago — fix is flexible narrative. Bridge rounds are extractive and under-discussed; founders facing down-round, M&A, or profitability cuts should get creative buying out investors and converting to common.

> **F2** Billion dollar PDF and timeline-native pricing: A confident worldview document at the right moment sets capital flows — venture rounds, public moves, and policy increasingly originate on X's unified feed where ≈500 tweets/day reach hundreds of millions and algorithms select entertaining, somewhat-correct posts that managers price from. Giffon argues markets lack nuance; Mag7 names trade nearly 100% 52-week ranges despite scale, so obvious long-Mag7 may beat cleverness.

> **F3** Software economics inversion — strings to compute: Per-seat SaaS sold copies of strings at ~zero marginal cost, producing high gross margins that rarely became net margins until PE extraction. AI products sell compute every inference — marginal cost is not zero, gross margins compress, and returns accrue to scale (Mag7, hyperscalers, cap-ex-heavy AI). Giffon's portfolio is 60%+ non-pure-software by value as capital seeks sponges beyond B2B SaaS capacity limits.

## Mental Model · *Billion Dollar PDF × Timeline Native × Compute-Era Margins*

> **Components**
>
> Private markets sell decade-long narratives; crystallize the story before returns arrive. Institutions survive by being reactive and reflexive to the timeline — monitoring posts, then posting actions that move the feed again. Software winners shift from high-margin copies to low-margin compute at massive scale; capital floods cap-ex-heavy categories (AI labs, hardware) when venture cannot absorb excess liquidity.
>
> **Application**
>
> Founders should optimize optionality in volatile periods — raise less, avoid ratchets, widen investor mandates — because commitment beats optionality in stable eras but not now. Public investors should expect narrative-driven marginal pricing on even the largest names and weigh whether SaaS selloffs reflect business-model obsolescence or timeline panic.

## Key Insights

> **1.** Capital follows the ball, not the scoreboard.
>
> **Q** What is the billion dollar PDF?
>
> **A** Someone crystallizes uncertainty into a confident worldview at the right moment — a deck, memo, or viral post — and billions reorganize around it until the next PDF arrives. Venture LPs buy narrative for ten years; the best funds master that interim product.

> **2.** Poster class replaces billionaire priests.
>
> **Q** Who sets society's worldview now?
>
> **A** After scientists and billionaires, Giffon sees billionaires deferring to top posters — scarcity shifted from money to timeline attention. Peak billionaire podcast saturation means marginal Elon interview adds little; influential posters command rooms Tyler Cowen once owned.

> **3.** SaaS is a model, not a moat — compute changes the math.
>
> **Q** Why is software getting repriced?
>
> **A** Per-seat tools face model risk, but the deeper shift is selling compute each token instead of zero-marginal-cost software copies — future looks like razor-thin margins requiring Walmart or Mag7 scale, punishing mid-cap SaaS while capital soaks into AI infrastructure.

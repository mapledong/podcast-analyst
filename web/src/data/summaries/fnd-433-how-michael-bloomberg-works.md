## Key Facts

> **F1** Fire and stake: Gutfreund tells the 39-year-old his life at Salomon is finished. Most of 63 partners stay as employees of the new company; he and about a half-dozen are pushed. $10 million is the consolation. He would have stayed for any job — in 1979 Billy and John already moved him off sales and trading onto computers. He does not visit. Harvard case method is the only schooling he praises. Goldman and Salomon both recruit; Salomon offers $9,000 and he takes the hotter firm. Once finished, gone.

> **F2** Build: he puts $300,000 of the windfall in, then $4 million of his $10 million, with almost two dozen families on the line. They sell a product that does not exist and pick a six-month date. The promise expires Saturday, so delivery slips to Monday afternoon. The pitch: the first investment box where a normal person hits a key and gets an answer, including questions they did not know to ask. Later the terminal carries functions from more than 5,000 programmers. He builds hardware until someone else builds it better, then stays on content and analytics. Style is the same then and now: break it into pieces, assign the person best suited, get action. Others plan to plan.

> **F3** Scale and edge: a few years after founding, 1990s cash is large enough that he is giving away $200 million a year; early 2000s about $700 million; Senra says the lifetime philanthropic number is over $10 billion. Ownership stays around 98–100%. Expansion rule: start with a little capital you can lose and a few people you can reassign — Time Warner/AOL is the anti-pattern. 'We do not want fair fights.' Jobs's brick line is the rhyme: don't lose faith, don't look back.

## Mental Model · *Once Finished Gone × Fake the Date × Unfair Fight*

> **Components**
>
> Treat a shove as working capital. Do not visit the old firm. Put your own money in until families depend on you. Sell a date, then work weekends to hit Monday afternoon. Never confuse the device with the business — hardware is a temporary wrapper around answers. Break problems into pieces and act; planning to plan is the enemy. Expand with money you can lose. Enter fights with an edge, not a fair field. Keep the method boring enough to repeat every week. Numbers in the room beat stories in the hallway. The founder who will not walk the floor, make the tenth call, or rewrite the Saturday software is already the cartel.
>
> **Application**
>
> Private:Bloomberg is the named terminal and the still-private cash machine. GS is the rejected $9,000-versus-hot-firm fork, not a short. AAPL is the Jobs rhyme on the brick, not an iPhone print. Fade fair-fight decks. Size any data terminal as content first, glass second.

## Key Insights

> **1.** The shove is the gift.
>
> **Q** What did $10 million buy?
>
> **A** A chance to stop looking back. He would have stayed for any Salomon job. He never went back.

> **2.** Ship the Monday.
>
> **Q** How do you sell a machine that does not exist?
>
> **A** Pick a six-month Saturday, slip to Monday afternoon, and rewrite the software until it boots.

> **3.** No fair fights.
>
> **Q** How does a small shop beat bigger ones?
>
> **A** Enter with an advantage. Start experiments with capital you can lose. Keep 98%.

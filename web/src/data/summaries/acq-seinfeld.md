## Key Facts

> **F1** Setup: Jerry is already a rare-air stand-up, which is the leverage Shapiro and West take to NBC. He flies back to New York and finds Larry David at Catch a Rising Star. Castle Rock — five guys plus $30 million of Coca-Cola money through Columbia — becomes the studio after an earlier ABC near-miss. Rob Reiner pushes Jason Alexander for George because Jerry is not the actor; Michael Richards auditions standing on his head. NBC will not promise a full season at first (13, not 24). Season 3 still averages 18 million viewers, a prime-time number Ben says you do not see anymore. The 100-episode unofficial syndication gate is the business plan hiding under the coffee-shop talk.

> **F2** First-run versus back end: early episode talent money is small; later Jerry is $1 million an episode on paper and then $5 million; the four are $600,000 each in the big renegotiation. A season can print $150 million of profits against a $200 million cost stack on the tape's later-year math. Jason Alexander's lifetime guild residuals are about $4 million — peanuts next to ownership. Castle Rock sells to Ted Turner / TBS in December 1993 for $100 million cash plus another $30 million as Fin-Syn repeal is coming (effective 1995): the last independent-studio window is closing, so they sell the factory that owns Seinfeld.

> **F3** Syndication event: 1995 local-affiliate sales — roughly a station in every market — clear over $500 million for the first five-year window, broadcast only, no cable yet. 1998, with the show still running, the renewed affiliate + TBS cable + international pile is reported at $1.1 billion; cumulative syndication is already $1.6 billion before the last episode. The finale (May 1998) draws 76.3 million viewers, 58% of that night. NBC sells 30-second spots for as much as $2 million, about 54% more than the $1.3 million Super Bowl rate the same year. Ben: that $110 million finale hour is 11% of NBC's total something on the tape — the network is renting a show it does not own.

> **F4** Streaming is just another syndication window. Hulu, 2015: $150 million for five years. Jerry's personal Netflix deal for Comedians in Cars (from Crackle) lands in 2017. The 2019 Seinfeld Netflix license is reported at well more than $500 million over five years — Ben calls it the largest sum a streamer has paid an outside studio for a series no longer in production, $100 million-plus a year, decades after cameras stopped. Friends and The Office print $425 million and $500 million five-year compares on the same tape. In 2026 the show is still in 60 million American homes, then 72 million by end-August in their update — almost finale-sized reach with no new episode.

> **F5** Who owns the joke: Fin-Syn barred networks from owning prime-time they aired, so profits sat with Castle Rock / the creators, not NBC. Ben analogizes the cap table to a Valley startup: most shows fail; the ones that work are power laws for individuals. Seinfeld is one of the last shows out of that system. Sony Pictures is the current distributor (and still sells DVD players — Ben's $100 research gag). Comcast's NBC is the original shelf, not the library. Creator belief — they would not change the no-growth, no-hug ending — is the product story. The business story is 180 episodes of people who already loved it, re-licensed forever.

## Mental Model · *Fin-Syn Cap Table × 100-Episode Gate × Streaming Window*

> **Components**
>
> Keep ownership off the network while Fin-Syn lasts. Survive the 13-episode order until Season 3's 18 million viewers. Cross ≈100 episodes so every local station can strip you. Sell the first five-year window for $500 million-plus, then cable/international for another billion. When SVOD arrives, auction the same 180 hours to Hulu and Netflix at sitcom-record rents. Creators on the cap table get the power law; actors get guild residuals. Turner buying Castle Rock is the studio cashing the factory as the legal window closes.
>
> **Application**
>
> NFLX is the 2019 $500 million-plus dead-show bid and the 2026 60–72 million still-watching print — a library check, not a comedy-development thesis. CMCSA/NBC is the network that paid $2 million finale spots and never owned the backend. SONY is the distributor of the tape and the current rights holder to underwrite, not a new-show studio multiple. Do not value a modern network comedy like 1995 Fin-Syn.

## What Makes It Work

> What works is not 'funny four people in a diner.' It is a star comic with walk-away leverage, a partner (Larry) who will not sand the edges, a production company that owns the negative, and a legal regime that forbids the network from taking that negative. Castle Rock's Coke money and later Turner sale are the cap-table events. The 100-episode custom plus local exclusives created a $500 million day in 1995 that first-run fees could not. Streaming repeated the auction: Hulu $150 million, Netflix well over $500 million, still the record for a finished series. Friends and Office money on the same tape shows the analog is other libraries, not new Netflix comedies at $10 million an episode. The finale's 76.3 million and Super Bowl-beating ad rates are the peak first-run print; 72 million Americans in 2026 are the duration print. NBC could sell the air. It could not keep the asset. Sony can. The slow-burn fan base is the asset: they did not widen the joke to chase the first 13-episode order, and that same stubbornness is why 72 million people still have it on in 2026.

>
> **Q** Why doesn't NBC own Seinfeld?
>
> **A** Financial Interest and Syndication rules kept networks off the backend. Castle Rock and the creators kept the show. Turner bought the studio for $100 million plus $30 million as repeal arrived.

> **2.** Syndication dwarfs first run.
>
> **Q** Where was the money?
>
> **A** 1995 local window >$500 million; 1998 cycle $1.1 billion; $1.6 billion booked before the finale. Residuals for the cast ≈$4 million lifetime.

> **3.** The finale was a Super Bowl for NBC.
>
> **Q** What did May 1998 print?
>
> **A** 76.3 million viewers, 58% of the night, $2 million spots versus $1.3 million for the 1998 Super Bowl — on a library the network did not own.

> **4.** Netflix paid a dead-show record.
>
> **Q** What is the 2019 check?
>
> **A** Well over $500 million for five years, the largest streamer license for a series no longer in production. Hulu was $150 million in 2015.

> **5.** 2026 reach is still finale-sized.
>
> **Q** Is the library decaying?
>
> **A** 60 million Americans, then 72 million by their August update — almost the finale audience, decades after cameras stopped.

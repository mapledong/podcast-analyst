## Key Facts

> **F1** The ladder. Chung's father had a healthy body and a diligent character and did not take days off. From age 10, Chung worked beside him and did not complain. The late 1930s rice-shop job paid a bag of rice a month. He treated small deliveries as if they were the whole job. The owner eventually offered to sell him the shop. He then ran an auto repair garage, including cars converted to charcoal and carbide when fuel was scarce, and then a construction company. Workers fled to Busan and he built them quarters. He does not give workers an excuse to procrastinate, and he does not give executives a separate elevator. A carpet going into a temporary site office, under a motto of diligence, frugality, and affection, is the scene he shuts down. Asked how he became the richest man in Korea, he answers with the sequence: rice shop, garage, construction, each time pushing again. The diligent, he says, lead lives 100 times more productive than the lazy. One day of diligence is a night of sleep. A year, two years, ten years, a life, is how the work gets recognized.

> **F2** The shipyard. A great idea starts as a grain of rice and is nurtured until he can see the project. In the early 1960s that project is Korea's first shipyard, and he had never built a boat. He travels for money. Japanese and American capital mock the technology. He quits in front of President Park. Park turns to the vice prime minister and says to reject all of Chung's proposals and never do business with him again, The line that brings Chung back is that a president and an economics minister had promised support and Chung was rolling over. He then has a Greek buyer. The purchase order and the down payment exist before the yard does. He also picks up a second order, an oil tanker, on the same trip. The government that had threatened him then tells him to build. The order is the idea, the buyer, the deposit, then the yard.

> **F3** The philosophy he wants repeated. Time is capital distributed equally. Success, if he has it, is using that capital on conviction. The poor man works 10 to 20 times harder than the rich man, because Korea did not have the resources of the advanced nations, only effort. Hyundai is his evidence. He plans every detail and then implements so fast it looks reckless. He does not experience himself as underprivileged, including farm work on an empty stomach. Positive thinking is a section of the book, not a slogan at the end. He does not trust lazy people. The bulldozer nickname, he thinks, was meant to mark an uneducated upstart who dug first and thought later. His reply is that the thinking happened before the digging, and that school is not the only place wisdom is stored. The modern group, he writes, could never have been built if he had accepted the nickname.

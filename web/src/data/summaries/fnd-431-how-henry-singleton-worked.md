## Key Facts

> **F1** Scoreboard: 20.4% annual compound 1963–1990; $1 → $180 when he stepped down. Munger: returns a mile higher than anyone else's, utterly ridiculous. Buffett: most CEOs rise through marketing/production/engineering/politics and then must allocate capital they have never practiced. Singleton gave most of his attention to deploying cash, not running plants. Avoided dividends, emphasized cash flow over reported earnings, never split the stock, refused the analyst circuit (the Sphinx).

> **F2** Two regimes: 1961–69 he purchased 130 companies (aviation electronics, specialty metals, insurance). All but two were bought with Teledyne's high-PE 1960s paper — conglomerates were the era's internet stocks. After the multiple collapsed he inverted: 1972 he announced a tender because 'our stock is simply too cheap' and he could earn more buying shares than almost anything else. Buybacks were then uncommon and treated as a lack of internal projects. He kept tendering until he had over 90% of the shares.

> **F3** Same toolkit as Buffett: both saw insurance float as investable capital and made insurance the largest, most important business. Decentralized operations; headquarters did capital allocation. Frugality stories (poker-parlor lunches, making the junior pick up the check) are the culture print. WWII and 1950s guidance work is the technical biography before the conglomerate — he was a world-class mathematician who programmed MIT's first computer and played chess blindfolded.

## Mental Model · *Issue Dear / Buy Cheap × Float × Sphinx HQ*

> **Components**
>
> Use expensive stock as currency when the multiple is silly (130 deals, 1961–69). When the stock is cheap, tender until you own the company (90%+). Treat insurance float as the real capital account. Decentralize operations so HQ only allocates. Ignore analysts. Never split, skip the dividend, report cash. Most CEOs were promoted for a different sport — do not copy their allocation.
>
> **Application**
>
> TDY is the living remnant of the machine, not a 1960s replay. BRK.B is the parallel float/allocation story Senra cites. Fade CEOs who only run operations and outsource the balance sheet. A buyback is a project when the stock is the cheapest asset in the room. Do not pay conglomerate-era PE for a roll-up that cannot invert into repurchase.

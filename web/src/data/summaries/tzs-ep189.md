## Background

> Practical Investments ep.189 (published ≈2026-06-28) is Yongqing's dialogue with Haige, a semiconductor veteran who started in foreign telecom equipment, then moved across end-device consumer electronics, factories, and server/data-center product lines — mostly P&L, BD, and strategy rather than R&D. They walk twenty years of Chinese and global chip cycles: smartphone multi-camera and optics booms, pandemic demand spikes, memory bankruptcies and reorganizations that later printed enormous profits, x86 versus ARM architecture politics (including Haiguang's AMD Zen IP path), and today's AI server stack where GPU landlords enjoy 70–80% gross margins while hyperscalers (and Chinese peers) build captive silicon. The investment half contrasts auction-market pricing in strong cycles with process tools for retail capital — convertibles, rules-based high-dividend baskets, and buying quality names after 60–70% drawdowns with room for 80%.

## Key Facts

> **F1** Cycle anatomy: semiconductors still show boom-bust capacity cycles; strong cycles shift from normal pricing to 'auctioning' future wafers, while weak cycles look like oversupply and price wars familiar from other Chinese industrial sectors. Memory history is extreme — players endured losses, spinouts (e.g. Kioxia/Western Digital paths in the discussion), then later years earning on the order of a trillion RMB — so trailing multiples without cycle context mislead (a '5× P/E' can be cheap or a trap).

> **F2** AI stack tightness: looking one-to-two years out, Haige ranks HBM among the tightest links because only three major suppliers matter, Micron is smaller, and capacity adds decided earlier imply little new supply until late next year at earliest; long-term contracts help but can break. GPU/platform vendors at 70–80% gross margins incentivize cloud and Chinese internet giants (Baidu Kunlun, Alibaba T-Head, ByteDance, etc.) to design captive chips the way Google/Amazon did.

> **F3** Second-half framing and process: both hosts reject 'just beginning' hype (SoftBank-style) without calling an ending — 'second half can last a long time.' Retail toolkit emphasized: convertibles as aligned with controlling shareholders who prefer not to repay cash; diversified high-dividend baskets; buying quality after deep drawdowns; avoid inverted-pyramid adds after a double when you do not understand the name (e.g. 70% HBM stories).

## Key Insights

> **1.** Strong cycles auction the future; weak cycles look like every other overbuilt industry.
>
> **Q** How should investors feel a chip cycle in real time?
>
> **A** Haige describes boom states as racing to lock future capacity and bust states as oversupply price wars. Valuation only makes sense across a full cycle — not a single 'cheap' multiple printed at the peak of earnings.

> **2.** HBM scarcity is a three-player problem for the next year or two.
>
> **Q** Where is the near-term bottleneck?
>
> **A** With SK hynix, Samsung, and a smaller Micron, and limited earlier expansion decisions, Haige expects tightness into next year if multimodal model demand keeps growing — contracts help but are not guarantees.

> **3.** Call it a second half, not a tip or a finale — and trade with process.
>
> **Q** How should retail size AI-chip excitement?
>
> **A** Yongqing and Haige prefer convertibles, high-dividend rules, and deep-drawdown quality buys over faith after a double. A long second half can still be profitable; inverted pyramids on unknown HBM stories are how accounts break.

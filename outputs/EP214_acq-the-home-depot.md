## Key Facts

> **F1** Origin: 1972 Daylin makes Bernie CEO of Handy Dan with no equity; he brings Arthur Blank as CFO. Sandy Sigoloff fires Bernie, Arthur, and audit manager Ron Brill in April 1978 on a labor-pretense. Langone, who had won the EDS IPO for Ross Perot as a young banker, tells Bernie he just got the golden horseshoe. They copy Pat Farah's Homeco warehouse in LA — 130,000 square feet — and open two Atlanta stores the same day, June 22, 1979, after a missed newspaper ad. Associates hand out free $1 bills in the parking lot. First-year volume prints about $7 million. Orange is leftover construction paint used as signage. They borrow 500 empty Delmar cabinet boxes the week before opening so the aisles look stacked to the ceiling.

> **F2** Capital: Langone's seed is $2 million for about 70% of the company. Perot looks at the same check, then walks — Ben and David price the Cadillac/control fight at about $223 billion of forgone value if he had held through today's $350 billion cap (original seed holders who stayed are still about 91% of their starting percentage after later dilution and buybacks). 1980 burns the first $2 million. They target $6 million, sell only $3 million, and raise about $4 million in the pre-IPO round. September 1981 IPO prices a $32 million market cap. $1,000 at IPO with dividends reinvested is ≈$17 million today versus ≈$170,000 in the S&P 500; they call it the number-one 45-year total-return name in the index, ahead of Apple's IPO dollar.

> **F3** Machine: SKU count is the point — low gross-margin percentages are fine if turns and gross-margin dollars feed the warehouse. Associates on salary (assistant store manager up) get options; hourly staff get a 15% discounted purchase plan with a no-risk floor in the early years. Inverted pyramid: customers and associates sit above a tiny CEO. Culture repair after Nardelli is Blake putting that pyramid back. 470,000 employees — more than any US big-tech name except Amazon. A little over half of revenue is pro contractors; the rest is DIY. Gross margin a little above 33%, operating margin 12.5%, net income $14 billion on the current year they quote, about an 8.5% net margin.

> **F4** Modern P&L: about 2,400 stores, ≈15 new boxes a year, 90% owned real estate. Blake-era years with almost no new stores still take revenue from $70 billion to $130 billion and net income from $4 billion to $11 billion; sales per store go from about $30 million to $65 million over 11 years. They sell HD Supply to PE for $8.3 billion in 2007 and stare back at the core box. COVID: $110 billion to about $160 billion in three years, then a hangover and a climb. US residential improvement spend: $28 billion just before founding, $47 billion five years later, about $600 billion now. Home Depot did not just take share of a fixed hardware aisle — it made weekend projects a category.

> **F5** People after the founders: Langone is on GE's board and recruits Bob Nardelli after the GE succession with the same horseshoe line. Nardelli is the centralization/adjacent-growth chapter. Frank Blake (2007–2014) is the recession CEO and a source for this tape; he and Ken pull attention back to store productivity. Company lifers follow: Menear runs it into the 2020s; Ted Decker takes over in 2022. Versus Lowe's, IKEA, and grocery, Ben's size compare is market cap and headcount, not a merchandising tie. Amazon is the only US tech employer they put above 470,000. The $100,000 kitchen ticket and the pro desk are the same flywheel as the 1979 $1-bill stunt: get the truck in the lot and sell the cart.

## Mental Model · *Warehouse Turns × Inverted Pyramid × Owned Boxes*

> **Components**
>
> Fill a box five times a hardware store and make the customer believe every SKU is in stock — even if you borrowed empty cartons the first week. Pay associates like owners (options and a 15% discount plan). Keep the CEO at the point of an upside-down pyramid under customers and floor staff. Own the dirt (90%). When adjacencies (HD Supply) distract, sell them and raise sales per store. Pro weekly tickets plus DIY weekends are two frequencies on one inventory pile. Timing: a $28 billion US repair market that becomes $600 billion is the hidden partner.
>
> **Application**
>
> Underwrite HD as owned real estate plus pro/DIY turns, not as a 15-store growth story. Lowe's is the smaller same-aisle compare, not a different model. Treat Nardelli-style centralization as the thing Blake had to unwind. Amazon is a labor and last-mile compare, not a replacement for a $100,000 kitchen cart. Perot's $2 million walk is the reminder that control fights on seed preferred can be the whole outcome.

---

## What Makes It Work

> The edge is a warehouse people will drive to for a full project, staffed by associates who can sell a cart instead of a SKU, on land the company owns. Handy Dan and the old hardware counter could not hold 130,000 square feet or the pro's weekly list. Lowe's copied the box later and still sits smaller on the tape's market-cap and employee compare. Grocery and IKEA are size jokes, not substitutes. The culture mechanism is the inverted pyramid plus equity for salaried floor bosses — Nardelli showed what happens when a GE operator flips it. COVID proved the capacity was already built: $50 billion of extra revenue in three years without a new chain. Buying frenzy and gross-margin dollars, not a high percentage, pay for ceiling-high inventory. Orange aprons and the pro desk are the same 1979 parking-lot habit: make the store a destination. Seven-powers language on this tape is process (how you merchandize and staff a warehouse) plus scale in a $600 billion US repair pile they helped create, financed by 45 years of 25% compounding and buybacks that left seed holders near their original 70% economics.
